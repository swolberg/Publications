Problem Statement¶

Business Context¶

As organizations grow, business analysts increasingly face the challenge of navigating large volumes of reports, research papers, and strategic documents. Extracting the right insights from lengthy materials can be time-consuming and overwhelming, especially when these insights directly influence key business decisions.

Consider joining a venture capital firm like Andreessen Horowitz and being assigned a dense report such as Harvard Business Review’s “How Apple is Organized for Innovation.” Manually reviewing such documents requires significant effort, slowing down the analysis process and increasing the chances of missing important details.

To overcome this information overload, businesses can leverage Semantic Search and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) models. These systems allow analysts to ask natural-language questions like, “How does Apple structure its teams for innovation?” and instantly retrieve relevant, accurate insights from the source document.

By integrating such AI-driven retrieval systems, organizations can streamline research workflows, reduce manual effort, and enable analysts to focus on high-value strategic thinking, ultimately improving decision-making speed and quality.

Common Questions to Answer

  1. Who are the authors of this article and who published this article?

  2. List down the three leadership characteristics in bulleted points and explain each one of the characteristics under two lines.

  3. Can you explain specific examples from the article where Apple's approach to leadership has led to successful innovations?

Objective¶

As an AI specialist, your task is to develop a RAG-based application that enables business analysts to efficiently extract insights from extensive business reports such as “How Apple is Organized for Innovation.” The objective is to understand the challenges of navigating long, information-dense documents, apply retrieval-augmented generation techniques to surface only the most relevant content, analyze how this improves the speed and accuracy of report interpretation, evaluate its potential to enhance strategic decision-making and productivity for analysts, and create a functional prototype that demonstrates the system’s effectiveness in answering queries, summarizing key insights, and supporting natural-language interactions without requiring users to read the entire report.

Data Description¶

“How Apple is Organized for Innovation” is a detailed Harvard Business Review article that examines Apple’s unique approach to structuring teams, driving innovation, and maintaining a culture of excellence. The article is provided as a PDF consisting of 11 pages, offering in-depth insights into Apple’s organizational design, leadership principles, and decision-making processes.

Observations — Baseline LLM¶

In this section, the three project questions were sent directly to the OpenAI API using the gpt-4o-mini model. The model generated responses based only on its pretrained knowledge and the wording of each question.

At this stage:

  • The Apple HBR PDF had not yet been loaded into the model.
  • No retrieval process or vector database was used.
  • No document chunks were supplied as context.
  • No Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) workflow had been implemented.
  • The responses therefore represent a baseline LLM-only approach.

The responses were evaluated for accuracy, relevance, completeness, formatting, and adherence to the user’s instructions.

  • Question 1 required precise factual information about the article’s authors and publisher. Although the model generated an answer, the response could not yet be directly verified or traced back to the assigned HBR document.

  • Question 2 tested instruction-following by requiring exactly three leadership characteristics in bullet form with brief explanations. This provides a useful baseline for evaluating whether prompt engineering improves formatting, conciseness, and compliance with the requested structure.

  • Question 3 required the model to connect Apple’s leadership approach with specific examples of successful innovation. The baseline model was able to generate explanatory content, but because it had not been given the source article, some examples or relationships may reflect the model’s general pretrained knowledge rather than information specifically retrieved from the assigned document.

Because no source document or retrieved context was provided, the baseline responses currently have no source traceability and carry a greater risk of unsupported claims or hallucination. The model may produce plausible and coherent answers, but those answers cannot yet be confirmed as grounded in the HBR article.

The generated responses were stored in base_prompt_response_1, base_prompt_response_2, and base_prompt_response_3 for later comparison.

Overall, this baseline establishes a benchmark for the next stages of the project. Prompt engineering will be evaluated to determine whether more detailed instructions and parameter tuning improve response structure, relevance, and instruction-following. RAG will then be evaluated to determine whether retrieving relevant information directly from the HBR document improves factual grounding, reliability, and source traceability.

Observations — LLM with Prompt Engineering¶

In this stage, the same gpt-4o-mini model was used, but the interaction was improved through a detailed system prompt and lower generation temperature. This allows the effect of prompt engineering to be compared more fairly with the baseline LLM approach because the underlying model remained unchanged.

The system prompt explicitly instructed the model to behave as a careful business research assistant and emphasized accuracy, relevance, clarity, conciseness, and adherence to the requested format. It also introduced safeguards such as avoiding unsupported claims and acknowledging uncertainty when necessary.

Improvements Compared with the Baseline LLM¶

The baseline approach sent each question directly to the model with no additional instructions about how the answer should be structured or how cautiously the model should handle factual information. In contrast, the prompt-engineered approach introduced several improvements:

  • Greater control over response structure: The system prompt explicitly instructed the model to follow the requested format. For Question 2, it was specifically directed to provide exactly three bullet points and keep each explanation brief.

  • Stronger emphasis on factual accuracy: The model was told not to invent unsupported information and to acknowledge uncertainty when appropriate. This helps reduce overly confident responses when the model may not have sufficient knowledge.

  • Improved relevance and conciseness: The prompt instructed the model to answer the question directly and prioritize relevance over creativity, reducing the likelihood of unnecessary or tangential information.

  • More consistent analytical behavior: The same system-level instructions applied to all three questions, giving the model a consistent role and response style across the entire section.

  • Reduced randomness: The temperature was lowered from 0.7 in the baseline model to 0.2 in the prompt-engineered model. This makes the responses more deterministic, focused, and appropriate for factual business analysis.

  • Better instruction-following: The system prompt provided explicit rules for each type of question, including formatting requirements for the leadership characteristics and an instruction to connect leadership practices with successful innovations.

Question-Level Observations¶

  • Question 1 requires precise factual information about the authors and publisher. Prompt engineering can encourage a more direct and cautious response, but the model is still relying on pretrained knowledge rather than the assigned article itself.

  • Question 2 benefits particularly from prompt engineering because the requested output has strict formatting requirements. The explicit instruction to provide exactly three bullet points with short explanations should improve compliance compared with the baseline approach.

  • Question 3 requires both factual recall and interpretation. The system prompt improves the likelihood that the model will explain the connection between Apple's leadership approach and specific innovation examples rather than simply listing examples.

Remaining Limitation of Prompt Engineering¶

Although prompt engineering improves how the model answers, it does not change where the model obtains its information.

At this stage, the Apple HBR PDF has still not been supplied to the model. The model is therefore answering from its pretrained knowledge rather than from the assigned document. As a result:

  • The answers cannot yet be directly verified against the source article.
  • There is still no source or page-level traceability.
  • Some facts may be incomplete, outdated, or based on knowledge outside the assigned HBR report.
  • The model may still produce a plausible but unsupported answer despite being instructed not to hallucinate.

This highlights an important distinction: prompt engineering can reduce the risk of poor responses, but it cannot guarantee factual grounding in a specific source document.

How RAG Is Expected to Improve the Results¶

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) addresses this remaining limitation by supplying the model with relevant passages retrieved directly from the Apple HBR document before generating an answer.

The RAG stage is expected to improve the solution in several ways:

  • Document grounding: Answers can be based on the actual HBR article rather than solely on pretrained model knowledge.
  • Improved factual reliability: Relevant source passages provide evidence for the generated response.
  • Reduced hallucination risk: The model is constrained by retrieved document context.
  • Source traceability: Retrieved chunks and page metadata can show where information originated.
  • Better handling of document-specific questions: Information that may not be reliably represented in the model's general knowledge can be retrieved directly from the assigned report.
  • Greater business trust: Analysts can validate generated insights against the original source before using them in decision-making.

Overall, prompt engineering improves the quality, structure, consistency, and instruction-following of the LLM's responses compared with the baseline. However, RAG is needed to improve the grounding, verifiability, and source traceability of those responses. This creates a logical progression from a general-purpose LLM, to a better-controlled LLM, and finally to a source-grounded RAG application.

WELL KNOWN FOR ITS innovations in hardware, software, 
and services. Thanks to them, it grew from some 8,000 
employees and $7 billion in revenue in 1997, the year Steve 
Jobs returned, to 137,000 employees and $260 billion in 
revenue in 2019. Much less well known are the organizational 
design and the associated leadership model that have played 
a crucial role in the company’s innovation success.
When Jobs arrived back at Apple, it had a conventional 
structure for a company of its size and scope. It was divided 
into business units, each with its own P&L responsibilities. 
General managers ran the Macintosh products group, the 
information appliances division, and the server products 
division, among others. As is often the case with decentral-
ized business units, managers were inclined to fight with 
one another, over transfer prices in particular. Believing that 
conventional management had stifled innovation, Jobs, in 
his first year returning as CEO, laid off the general managers 
of all the business units (in a single day), put the entire com-
pany under one P&L, and combined the disparate functional 
departments of the business units into one functional organi-
zation. (See the exhibit “Apple’s Functional Organization.”)
The adoption of a functional structure may have been 
un­surprising for a company of Apple’s size at the time. What is 
surprising—in fact, remarkable—is that Apple retains it today, 
even though the company is nearly 40 times as large in terms 
of revenue and far more complex than it was in 1998. Senior 
vice presidents are in charge of functions, not products. As 
was the case with Jobs before him, CEO Tim Cook occupies the 
only position on the organizational chart where the design, 
engineering, operations, marketing, and retail of any of Apple’s 
main products meet. In effect, besides the CEO, the company 
operates with no conventional general managers: people 
who control an entire process from product development 
through sales and are judged according to a P&L statement.
Business history and organizational theory make the case 
that as entrepreneurial firms grow large and complex, they 
must shift from a functional to a multidivisional structure to 
align accountability and control and prevent the congestion 
that occurs when countless decisions flow up the org chart 
to the very top. Giving business unit leaders full control over 
key functions allows them to do what is best to meet the 
needs of their individual units’ customers and maximize 
their results, and it enables the executives overseeing them 
to assess their performance. As the Harvard Business School 
historian Alfred Chandler documented, U.S. companies such 
as DuPont and General Motors moved from a functional to 
a multidivisional structure in the early 20th century. By the 
latter half of the century the vast majority of large corpora-
tions had followed suit. Apple proves that this conventional 
approach is not necessary and that the functional structure 
may benefit companies facing tremendous technological 
change and industry upheaval.
Apple’s commitment to a functional organization does 
not mean that its structure has remained static. As the 
importance of artificial intelligence and other new areas has 
increased, that structure has changed. Here we discuss the 
innovation benefits and leadership challenges of Apple’s 
distinctive and ever-evolving organizational model, which 
may be useful for individuals and companies wanting to 
better understand how to succeed in rapidly changing 
environments.

WHY A FUNCTIONAL ORGANIZATION?
Apple’s main purpose is to create products that enrich 
people’s daily lives. That involves not only developing 
entirely new product categories such as the iPhone and the 
Apple Watch, but also continually innovating within those 
categories. Perhaps no product feature better reflects Apple’s 
commitment to continuous innovation than the iPhone cam-
era. When the iPhone was introduced, in 2007, Steve Jobs 
devoted only six seconds to its camera in the annual keynote 
event for unveiling new products. Since then iPhone camera 
technology has contributed to the photography industry 
with a stream of innovations: High dynamic range imaging 
(2010), panorama photos (2012), True Tone flash (2013), opti-
cal image stabilization (2015), the dual-lens camera (2016), 
portrait mode (2016), portrait lighting (2017), and night mode 
(2019) are but a few of the improvements.
To create such innovations, Apple relies on a structure 
that centers on functional expertise. Its fundamental belief 
is that those with the most expertise and experience in a 
domain should have decision rights for that domain. This 
is based on two views: First, Apple competes in markets 
where the rates of technological change and disruption are 
high, so it must rely on the judgment and intuition of people 
with deep knowledge of the technologies responsible for 
disruption. Long before it can get market feedback and solid 
market forecasts, the company must make bets about which 
technologies and designs are likely to succeed in smart-
phones, computers, and so on. Relying on technical experts 
rather than general managers increases the odds that those 
bets will pay off.

def generate_rag_response(
    user_input,
    k=4,
    max_tokens=500,
    temperature=0,
    top_p=0.95
):
    global qna_system_message, qna_user_message_template

    # Retrieve relevant document chunks
    relevant_document_chunks = retriever.get_relevant_documents(
        query=user_input
    )

    # Preserve both content and page metadata
    context_list = []

    for d in relevant_document_chunks:
        page = d.metadata.get("page")

        # PyMuPDF commonly stores page numbers starting at 0
        if isinstance(page, int):
            page_number = page + 1
        else:
            page_number = "Unknown"

        context_list.append(
            f"[Source PDF Page {page_number}]\n{d.page_content}"
        )

    # Combine retrieved document chunks
    context_for_query = "\n\n".join(context_list)

    # Insert context and question into the prompt
    user_message = qna_user_message_template.replace(
        '{context}',
        context_for_query
    )

    user_message = user_message.replace(
        '{question}',
        user_input
    )

    # Generate the response
    try:
        response = client.chat.completions.create(
            model="gpt-4o-mini",
            messages=[
                {
                    "role": "system",
                    "content": qna_system_message
                },
                {
                    "role": "user",
                    "content": user_message
                }
            ],
            max_tokens=max_tokens,
            temperature=temperature,
            top_p=top_p
        )

        response = response.choices[0].message.content.strip()

    except Exception as e:
        response = f"Sorry, I encountered the following error:\n{e}"

    return response

Observations — Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)¶

What Was Done in the RAG Stage¶

The RAG implementation added a document-retrieval layer between the user's question and the language model. Unlike the baseline and prompt-engineered approaches, the model was no longer required to answer only from its pretrained knowledge.

The workflow consisted of the following steps:

  1. The 11-page Harvard Business Review article was loaded from Google Drive using PyMuPDFLoader.
  2. The document was divided into 18 text chunks using a token-based recursive text splitter.
  3. A chunk size of 700 tokens with 100 tokens of overlap was used to preserve contextual continuity between adjacent chunks.
  4. Each chunk was converted into a 1,536-dimensional embedding vector using the OpenAI embedding model.
  5. The embeddings and associated document metadata were stored in a Chroma vector database.
  6. For each user question, semantic similarity search retrieved the four most relevant document chunks (k=4).
  7. The retrieved passages, along with their PDF page metadata, were supplied to gpt-4o-mini as context.
  8. The model was explicitly instructed to answer only from the retrieved HBR content and to identify the supporting PDF page numbers where possible.

This changes the application from a general-purpose LLM into a document-grounded question-answering system.

Evidence That Semantic Retrieval Worked¶

The similarity search for Question 2 successfully retrieved the section of the article titled “Three Leadership Characteristics.”

The retrieved passage explicitly identified Apple's three expected leadership characteristics as:

  • deep expertise,
  • immersion in the details, and
  • willingness to collaboratively debate other functions during collective decision-making.

This is an important result because the semantic search did not simply match isolated keywords. It located the passage in the source document that directly addresses the conceptual meaning of the question.

The retrieved context also contained supporting information about Apple's functional organizational structure, the principle of experts leading experts, and the relationship between leadership expertise and innovation.

This demonstrates that the embedding and vector-search process was capable of identifying meaningful business context rather than relying solely on exact word matching.

Embedding and Vector Representation¶

The first two document chunks were successfully converted into numerical embedding vectors with:

  • Embedding 1 dimension: 1,536
  • Embedding 2 dimension: 1,536
  • Same dimension: True

The matching dimensions confirm that all document chunks are represented within the same vector space. This makes semantic similarity comparisons possible between a user's question and the stored document passages.

The value of embeddings is that the system can identify conceptually related information even when the wording in the user's question does not exactly match the wording in the document.

Source Traceability¶

A major improvement in the RAG implementation is the preservation of document metadata.

Each retrieved chunk contains information such as:

  • source document,
  • PDF page,
  • file path, and
  • document content.

The response function preserves this information by adding a label such as:

[Source PDF Page X]

before each retrieved passage.

This gives the language model information about where its evidence originated and allows the final answer to provide page-level traceability.

This is a major improvement over both earlier approaches because a business analyst can now verify an important generated insight against the original report.

Comparison with the Baseline LLM¶

The baseline LLM sent the questions directly to gpt-4o-mini without supplying the HBR article.

As a result:

  • answers depended entirely on pretrained model knowledge,
  • there was no guarantee that the information came from the assigned article,
  • source verification was not possible, and
  • hallucination risk was higher.

The baseline demonstrated whether the model already knew something about the topic, but it did not provide a reliable document-analysis workflow.

RAG improves this by retrieving the relevant HBR content before asking the model to formulate an answer.

Therefore, the key improvement is not merely that RAG may generate a more polished answer; it changes the evidence available to the model.

Comparison with Prompt Engineering¶

Prompt engineering improved the behavior of the same LLM by giving it detailed instructions about:

  • accuracy,
  • response structure,
  • conciseness,
  • formatting,
  • uncertainty, and
  • avoidance of unsupported claims.

Lowering the temperature from 0.7 to 0.2 also made the prompt-engineered responses more focused and deterministic.

However, prompt engineering still did not provide the model with the assigned HBR document.

This created an important limitation:

Prompt engineering improved how the model answered, but RAG improved what evidence the model could use to answer.

RAG retains the advantages of a carefully designed prompt while also adding retrieved source material.

Overall RAG Assessment¶

The RAG implementation provides the strongest approach of the three tested methods because it combines:

  • semantic retrieval,
  • prompt engineering,
  • source-document context,
  • deterministic generation,
  • metadata preservation, and
  • natural-language response generation.

The resulting architecture is better suited to business-document analysis because the model is no longer expected to recall every relevant fact from training. Instead, the system retrieves relevant evidence when it is needed and uses the LLM primarily to interpret, summarize, and communicate that evidence.

This directly supports the project objective of reducing the amount of time analysts spend manually searching long reports while improving the reliability and usefulness of the resulting insights.

Important Limitations¶

RAG improves factual grounding, but it does not eliminate all risk of error.

The quality of a RAG response depends heavily on the quality of retrieval. If the correct passage is not retrieved, the model cannot reliably use information that is absent from its context.

The similarity-search output also showed some duplicated or highly overlapping chunks. Although overlap helps preserve context, duplicate retrieval can consume context-window space without adding new information. A production implementation could improve this by:

  • deduplicating retrieved chunks,
  • testing different values of k,
  • using Maximum Marginal Relevance (MMR),
  • adding metadata filters, or
  • reranking retrieved passages before sending them to the LLM.

Therefore, RAG should be viewed as a major improvement in evidence-based generation, but not as an automatic guarantee of correctness.

Actionable Insights¶

1. RAG can significantly reduce manual document-review effort¶

The prototype demonstrates that semantic retrieval can locate relevant passages within a lengthy business report without requiring the analyst to manually read the document from beginning to end.

For organizations reviewing large volumes of research reports, market studies, investment documents, or internal reports, this could substantially reduce information-search time and allow analysts to focus more effort on interpretation and decision-making.

2. Source traceability is critical for business use¶

The most important advantage of the RAG approach is not simply faster answers; it is the ability to connect generated responses back to the original source.

Preserving PDF page metadata allows analysts to verify important claims before using them in an investment recommendation or strategic decision.

For high-impact business workflows, traceability should be treated as a core design requirement rather than an optional feature.

3. Prompt engineering and RAG solve different problems¶

The project demonstrates that prompt engineering and RAG are complementary.

Prompt engineering improves:

  • response structure,
  • instruction-following,
  • consistency,
  • conciseness, and
  • communication quality.

RAG improves:

  • source grounding,
  • document-specific factual accuracy,
  • retrieval of relevant evidence,
  • traceability, and
  • verifiability.

A production application should therefore combine both approaches rather than treating them as competing alternatives.

4. Retrieval quality determines downstream answer quality¶

The language model can only reason effectively over the information that has been successfully retrieved.

Therefore, document chunking, embeddings, retrieval strategy, the value of k, metadata quality, and passage reranking should be treated as important model-design parameters.

The retrieval stage should be evaluated independently rather than assuming that a fluent final answer means that retrieval was successful.

5. Apple's organizational model provides a strategic leadership insight¶

The retrieved article suggests that Apple connects expertise with decision rights rather than relying primarily on traditional general-management structures.

The report identifies three important leadership behaviors:

  • deep expertise,
  • immersion in details, and
  • collaborative debate.

For organizations operating in highly technical or rapidly changing markets, this suggests that decision authority may be more effective when placed closer to domain expertise.

6. Innovation may require protecting long-term product decisions from excessive short-term financial pressure¶

The retrieved content describes how Apple's functional organization enables technical leaders to weigh user value and innovation against cost rather than allowing short-term product-level financial targets to dominate every decision.

The iPhone 7 Plus dual-lens camera and portrait-mode example illustrates how expert-led organizations may be more willing to make technically informed investments whose value is uncertain at the time of development.

Business Recommendations¶

Recommendation 1 — Deploy RAG for research-intensive analyst workflows¶

Organizations that regularly analyze lengthy documents should consider RAG-based assistants for:

  • investment research,
  • competitive intelligence,
  • market reports,
  • earnings documents,
  • technical reports,
  • due-diligence materials, and
  • internal knowledge repositories.

The goal should be to augment analyst judgment rather than replace it.

Recommendation 2 — Preserve metadata and citations throughout the pipeline¶

Every document ingested into an enterprise RAG system should retain metadata such as:

  • document name,
  • page number,
  • section,
  • publication date, and
  • source location.

Generated responses should surface this metadata so analysts can verify important claims.

Recommendation 3 — Use human validation for high-impact decisions¶

RAG improves grounding but should not be treated as an autonomous decision-maker.

For investment, legal, financial, or strategic recommendations, users should verify material conclusions against the source documents before acting.

The system should therefore be designed as a decision-support tool, not as a replacement for expert judgment.

Recommendation 4 — Measure retrieval performance separately from generation quality¶

Organizations should evaluate whether the retriever is returning the correct passages before evaluating the quality of the final LLM response.

Useful evaluation measures could include:

  • retrieval precision,
  • retrieval recall,
  • answer relevance,
  • faithfulness to context,
  • citation accuracy, and
  • completeness.

A fluent response should not automatically be interpreted as an accurate response.

Recommendation 5 — Optimize retrieval before scaling¶

The current prototype uses similarity search with k=4, which performed well for the leadership-characteristics question.

Before deploying the system at enterprise scale, the organization should experiment with:

  • different chunk sizes,
  • different overlap values,
  • alternative embedding models,
  • different values of k,
  • MMR retrieval,
  • reranking, and
  • duplicate-chunk removal.

These changes could improve retrieval diversity and reduce irrelevant or repetitive context.

Recommendation 6 — Extend the prototype to multiple documents¶

The current solution analyzes a single HBR article. A production implementation should support collections of documents so analysts can ask questions across multiple reports simultaneously.

Additional capabilities could include:

  • document filtering,
  • date filtering,
  • company filtering,
  • source ranking,
  • automatic citations,
  • conversation history, and
  • side-by-side source comparison.

Overall Business Recommendation¶

The prototype demonstrates that RAG is a practical approach for reducing information overload in document-intensive business environments.

The greatest business value comes from combining the natural-language capabilities of an LLM with retrieval, provenance, and human verification.

For a venture-capital firm or similar research-driven organization, this approach could shorten the time required to review large collections of reports while allowing analysts to spend more time evaluating strategic implications, identifying patterns, and making higher-value decisions.
